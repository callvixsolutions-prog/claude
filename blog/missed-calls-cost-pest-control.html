What missed calls really cost a pest control business

By Callvix Solutions6 min readPublished 10 July 2026Updated 20 October 2026

Every pest control owner knows the feeling: you get out of a crawl space, check your phone, and see two missed calls. You call back — no answer. Maybe they found someone else; maybe they’ll try you again tomorrow. Your call log can’t tell you which. Not every missed call is a lost job, but some are — and you can estimate how much is at stake from your own records.

The short version: this article uses a simple model and your own records, not an industry statistic about voicemail. With four numbers from your own phone system and CRM, you can build a planning scenario for what unanswered calls could be worth — using your data, not someone else’s averages.

Why unanswered calls are worth reviewing

Two things make an unanswered call worth a closer look in pest control:

  • Urgency. Wasps by the door, rodents in the attic, ants across the kitchen — many callers want it handled today, and a caller who can’t get through may try another company before you call back.
  • Recurring value. Some first jobs turn into a recurring service plan, so a first call can be worth more than its first job. The estimate below counts the first job only.

Estimate the first-job revenue at stake

This is a planning scenario — not a forecast, and not a measure of revenue you have already lost. Pick a representative period (a month is easiest) and count each potential customer once.

  • A — Qualified unanswered callers. Unique callers in the period who reached no one and wanted a service you offer, in your area. Leave out spam, wrong numbers, repeat attempts by the same caller and existing customers calling about their account. If your call log can’t tell you who qualified, count those callers separately and run the scenario with and without them.
  • B — Share not recovered. The share of A that never booked with you — not through your callback, and not by calling back, booking online or reaching you another way — by a follow-up cutoff you state. Calls too recent to reach that cutoff are pending, not unrecovered. If you don’t track these outcomes, use an assumption and label it as one.
  • C — Assumed completed-job rate. The share of unrecovered qualified callers you estimate would have become a completed, paid first job if answered. Use your own answered-lead-to-paid-job rate as a starting point, and allow for cancellations and appointment capacity. It remains an assumption: callers who hung up won’t necessarily behave like callers who got through.
  • D — Average first-job revenue. What you actually collect on a typical first job — not a quoted price, and not a lifetime value.

First-job revenue at stake for the period ≈ A × B × C × D

Qualified unanswered callers (A)40
Not recovered by any route (B) — example figure50%
Assumed completed-job rate (C) — example figure40%

First-job revenue at stake for the period≈ $4,000

Run it twice if you’re unsure of an input — once cautious, once optimistic — and plan around the range. Then two checks. Revenue isn’t profit: compare the contribution margin on those additional paid jobs with the full extra coverage cost, including all calls routed to the service, not just the calls that become jobs. And one period is one period: don’t multiply it by 12 without looking at your seasonality, and don’t add recurring revenue unless your own retention figures support it. Callbacks, appointment capacity and why people called all move the real number, in either direction.

Where the calls are actually being missed

Three gaps are worth checking first:

  • During service hours, when you and your techs are on-site and can't get to the phone.
  • After hours and weekends, when the office is closed but pest problems aren't — where a written after-hours coverage and escalation plan decides what happens to each call.
  • During busy-season overflow, when calls come in faster than one person can answer. Our season-by-region guide to pest call volume shows when that usually happens.

How to plug the leak

Match the fix to the gap: overflow during busy periods, faster owned callbacks, clearer online booking, or extra live coverage during agreed hours. Compare the incremental paid work each change could support with its total cost.

Live coverage is one of those options. A pest control answering service answers in your company name during the hours you choose to cover, qualifies the issue and follows the booking rules agreed with you, while you’re in a crawl space or driving between stops.

Frequently asked questions

How many pest control leads are lost to missed calls?

This article uses a simple model and your own records rather than a published loss rate. Count the unique qualified callers who reached no one in a period, leaving out repeat attempts, spam, wrong numbers and existing customers calling about their account. Then check whether each one later reached you through a callback, online booking or another route, by a follow-up cutoff you state; calls too recent to reach that cutoff are pending, not unrecovered. Where qualification or recovery is unknown, treat it as an assumption. The result is a planning opportunity, not a count of lost jobs.

How do I calculate the cost of missed calls?

Count the unique qualified callers who reached no one (A), take the share that never booked with you by any route before your follow-up cutoff (B), apply the completed-job rate you assume for them (C), and multiply by your average collected first-job revenue (D). As an example only, with figures you would replace with your own, 40 × 50% × 40% × $500 is about $4,000 of first-job revenue at stake for the period. It’s a planning scenario, not a measure of revenue already lost.

Why might callers not leave a voicemail?

Pest problems can feel urgent. Some homeowners want help now rather than a callback later, so if no one answers they may try another company.

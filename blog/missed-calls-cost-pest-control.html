What missed calls really cost a pest control business

By Callvix Solutions6 min readPublished 10 July 2026Updated 13 October 2026

Every pest control owner knows the feeling: you get out of a crawl space, check your phone, and see two missed calls. You call back — no answer. Maybe they found someone else; maybe they’ll try you again tomorrow. Your call log can’t tell you which. Not every missed call is a lost job, but some are — and you can estimate how much is at stake from your own records.

The short version: we are not going to quote you an industry statistic about voicemail, because the numbers that circulate are mostly vendor-published and impossible to verify. What we can do is give you the arithmetic. With four numbers from your own phone system and CRM, you can build a planning scenario for what unanswered calls could be worth — using your data, not someone else’s averages.

Why unanswered calls are worth reviewing

Two things make an unanswered call worth a closer look in pest control:

  • Urgency. Wasps by the door, rodents in the attic, ants across the kitchen — many callers want it handled today, and a caller who can’t get through may try another company before you call back.
  • Recurring value. Some first jobs turn into a recurring service plan, so a first call can be worth more than its first job. The estimate below counts the first job only.

Estimate the first-job revenue at stake

This is a planning scenario — not a forecast, and not a measure of revenue you have already lost. Pick a representative period (a month is easiest) and count each potential customer once.

  • A — Qualified unanswered callers. Unique callers in the period who reached no one and wanted a service you offer, in your area. Leave out spam, wrong numbers, repeat attempts by the same caller and existing customers calling about their account. If your call log can’t tell you who qualified, count those callers separately and run the scenario with and without them.
  • B — Share not recovered. The share of A that did not become a booking through your normal callback. If you don’t track callback outcomes, use an assumption and label it as one.
  • C — Assumed booking rate. The share of B you think would have booked if a trained person had answered. Your booking rate on answered calls is a reasonable starting point, but it is an assumption: callers who hung up won’t necessarily behave like callers who got through.
  • D — Average first-job revenue. What you actually collect on a typical first job — not a quoted price, and not a lifetime value.

First-job revenue at stake for the period ≈ A × B × C × D

Run it twice if you’re unsure of an input — once cautious, once optimistic — and plan around the range. Then two checks. Revenue isn’t profit: compare the margin on the extra jobs with what additional coverage would cost. And one period is one period: don’t multiply it by 12 without looking at your seasonality, and don’t add recurring revenue unless your own retention figures support it. Callbacks, appointment capacity and why people called all move the real number, in either direction.

Where the calls are actually being missed

Three gaps are worth checking first:

  • During service hours, when you and your techs are on-site and can't get to the phone.
  • After hours and weekends, when the office is closed but pest problems aren't — where a written after-hours coverage and escalation plan decides what happens to each call.
  • During busy-season overflow, when calls come in faster than one person can answer.

How to plug the leak

The fix isn’t “try to answer faster” — you’re already busy running jobs. It’s having a capable person pick up during the hours you choose to cover, qualify the issue, and book the appointment by your rules. That’s what a pest control answering service does: within your coverage hours, your phone is answered in your company name while you’re in a crawl space or driving between stops.

Sources and methodology

This page deliberately contains no “industry average” missed-call statistic. The widely-quoted figures in this category are almost all published by companies selling call-answering software, are rarely reproducible, and are not specific to pest control — so we have left them out rather than repeat them.

  • Oldroyd, McElheran & Elkington, “The Short Life of Online Sales Leads,” Harvard Business Review, March 2011 — cited only for the general finding that firms typically respond far slower than inbound leads remain contactable. Its figures come from online B2B lead forms, not pest-control phone calls. Verified 10 September 2026.

The model: the calculation on this page is arithmetic (A × B × C × D), not a research finding. Every input should be your own. The worked example uses round placeholder numbers purely to show the shape of the calculation.

Frequently asked questions

How many pest control leads are lost to missed calls?

There is no reliable published figure specific to pest control, and the numbers that circulate widely are mostly vendor-published. The practical answer is to measure it yourself: count unanswered inbound calls in your phone system over a month and compare it against how many of those numbers ever became customers.

How do I calculate the cost of missed calls?

Count the qualified callers who reached no one (A), take the share you didn’t win back on a callback (B), apply the booking rate you assume for them (C), and multiply by your average collected first-job revenue (D). As an illustrative example only, 40 × 50% × 40% × $500 is about $4,000 of first-job revenue at stake for the period. It’s a planning scenario, not a measure of revenue already lost.

Why might callers not leave a voicemail?

Pest problems can feel urgent. Some homeowners want help now rather than a callback later, so if no one answers they may try another company.

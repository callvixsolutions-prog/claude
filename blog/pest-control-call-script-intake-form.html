How to use this script

Treat it as a decision structure, not a word-for-word recital. The wording in the blue boxes is a starting point you should rewrite in your own voice; the parts that matter are the order of the questions and the boundaries around what a handler may promise.

Read it once end to end, then fill in the four blanks in the next section. A script without those four decisions filled in will produce confident-sounding answers that your field team has to undo the next morning.

Fill these in before the phone rings

Everything below depends on four decisions that only you can make. Write them down and give them to whoever answers.

  1. Service area. The ZIP codes, cities or radius you actually serve, and what to do with an address just outside them — decline politely, refer, or capture for review.
  2. Booking authority. Which calendar slots a handler may offer without asking you, and which requests must be escalated instead of booked.
  3. Pricing boundaries. What a handler may say about price — a published starting range, or nothing at all beyond “a technician will confirm pricing.”
  4. Escalation contact. One named person, one contact method, and what counts as urgent enough to use it after hours.

If you have not settled the coverage windows those rules apply to, our after-hours coverage and escalation plan walks through choosing them from your own call data.

The call script, step by step

Before you start: safety overrides everything. If at any point the caller describes a medical emergency, a severe reaction to a sting or a possible pesticide exposure, stop the intake and route the call immediately — 911 if it may be life-threatening, the Poison Help line at 1-800-222-1222 otherwise. Give no medical, chemical or treatment advice. Document the call and report it the same day.

1. Opening greeting — in your company’s name

Say“Thank you for calling [Company Name], this is [First Name]. How can I help you today?”

Answer in the company’s name, not a generic greeting. If an outside service answers for you, the greeting should be indistinguishable from your own office. Give a first name — callers with an urgent pest problem settle faster when they know who they are talking to.

2. Caller name and a confirmed callback number

Ask“Can I start with your name and the best number to reach you on, in case we get cut off?”
Read back“Let me repeat that back — [number]. Is that right?”

Take this right after the greeting, before anything else. If the call drops mid-sentence you still have a lead. Always read the number back digit by digit; it catches errors before the call ends.

  • Caller name
  • Callback number (read back)
  • Email, if offered

3. New caller or existing customer?

Ask“Have we been out to your property before, or is this your first time calling us?”

This single question routes the whole call. An existing account may be covered by a re-service policy and should never be booked as a new one-time job by mistake. If you have authorized account access, check the existing record before making account-specific promises. If you do not, record the caller’s stated status and hand off any re-service, warranty or billing question for verification.

  • New lead
  • Existing customer
  • Record checked with authorized access / caller-stated status only

4. Service address, ZIP code and service-area check

Ask“What’s the address where you’re seeing the problem, including the ZIP code?”

Check the address against your service area before offering any appointment time. Offering a slot and withdrawing it is worse than a polite decline, and a job booked well outside your route costs more than the call was worth. If the address is borderline, capture it and escalate rather than guessing.

  • Street address
  • ZIP code
  • In area / out of area / borderline

5. The problem, in the caller’s own words

Ask“Tell me what you’re seeing — where in the property, and roughly how long it’s been going on?”

Write down what the caller says, not what you think it means. “Small black ants along the kitchen counter for two weeks” is useful to a technician. “Ant problem” is not, and “odorous house ants” is a guess the handler is not qualified to make.

  • Pest/problem as described
  • Location on property
  • How long / how many seen
  • Previous treatment attempts

Hard rule: no diagnosis, no treatment advice

Whoever answers the phone must not identify the pest, recommend a pesticide or treatment, estimate how severe an infestation is, or give medical advice — even when the caller pushes for an answer. In this intake workflow, record observations and leave identification or treatment recommendations to the company’s authorized pest professional.

A safe deflection: “I don’t want to guess at that. I’ll record what you’ve seen and follow our process for an authorized pest professional to review it.”

6. Residential or commercial

Ask“And is this a home, or a business property?”

Commercial work usually carries different pricing, scheduling, documentation and sometimes licensing requirements. Some companies route every commercial enquiry to an owner or manager rather than booking it at intake. Decide which you do, and put it in the script.

  • Residential
  • Commercial
  • Multi-unit / HOA

7. Urgency and the safety boundary

Ask“Is anyone in the home having a reaction, or is anything making the property unsafe right now?”

This question separates ordinary urgency — which is a scheduling problem — from a safety situation, which is not a booking at all. Most urgent-sounding calls are simply priority bookings: a stinging-insect nest by a door, or a commercial caller who needs the earliest standard visit.

  • Routine
  • Priority / time-sensitive
  • Safety — stop and route

Safety routing — stop the booking flow

If a caller describes a medical emergency, a severe allergic reaction to a sting, or a suspected pesticide poisoning, stop taking the booking and direct them to the right authority:

911 for anything life-threatening — collapse, seizure, trouble breathing, or someone who cannot be woken.

For a suspected pesticide exposure that is not immediately life-threatening, the national Poison Help line at 1-800-222-1222, or the online tool at poison.org.

Do not give medical, chemical or treatment advice. EPA advises reading and following the pesticide label’s instructions and safety warnings, so an intake handler should never improvise around a product label. Document who you routed the caller to, and tell your manager the same day.

8. Booking authority and pricing boundaries

Say (if you publish a range)“For a standard [service] on a home that size, our pricing usually starts around [range]. [Your pricing confirmation policy.]”
Say (if you do not)“I’m not able to quote that one myself — I’ll have [name] confirm the pricing with you. Can I take your details so they can follow up?”

Never invent a number. Use your actual pricing and confirmation policy here; do not promise an on-site final quote, a provisional visit or a free inspection unless that is the approved workflow. The common “never promise” list: a firm quote for non-standard work, a named technician, a guaranteed arrival time you cannot staff, a warranty decision, or a same-day visit outside your booking rules.

9. Booking the appointment

If the handler is authorized and the appointment has been reserved

Offer“I have [day] between [window], or [day] between [window]. Which works better?”
Confirm“You’re booked for [day, date, window] at [address]. [Use only the confirmation method your company actually provides.] Is there anything the technician needs to know about access?”

If the handler can only capture a request

Say“I’ve recorded your preferred [day/window]. This is a request, not a confirmed appointment. [Named team] will confirm the available time by [approved deadline and method].”

Use the first branch only after the slot has actually been reserved in the agreed source of truth. Do not promise text/email confirmations unless that workflow is available. Record both the status and the exact promise in the handoff. Either way, repeat the day, window and address, and capture access notes while the caller is still on the line.

  • Booked or requested
  • Date + arrival window
  • Access notes / gate code
  • Pets on site
  • Confirmation method, if one exists

10. Manager escalation

Say“That one needs [name] to confirm for you. I’m going to take the details now and have them call you back on [number] — is that the best number?”

Escalate rather than improvise whenever the call falls outside written rules: commercial accounts, termite or wildlife work, warranty and complaint calls, pricing outliers, and any address on the edge of the service area. Capture the full details first so the manager is not starting from zero, then log who was contacted and when.

11. Existing customers: reschedule and cancellation

Reschedule — authorized, new slot confirmed“I can move that for you. I have [option] or [option] — which suits?”
Reschedule — request only“I’ve noted that you’d like to move your visit to [preferred day/window]. [Named team] will confirm the change with you by [approved deadline and method].”
Cancellation“I’m sorry to hear that. Can I ask what’s prompting the change? I’ll pass your request to [name], who handles cancellations, and they’ll explain the next step under our policy.”

Reschedule only if the handler has authority, current availability and the account information needed to check technician, territory, duration and recurring-service restrictions. Otherwise capture the requested change and send it to the authorized team without changing or promising a slot. Handle cancellations the way your written policy says: if it routes them to an owner or manager, capture the reason in the caller’s words, tell the caller who will follow up, and pass it on the same day. Don’t refuse or delay a cancellation your policy allows the handler to process. If the handler completes it, confirm the outcome before ending the call. Only promise a callback time your team has approved and can meet. A recorded reason is retention data worth having, and it is easy to lose if nobody writes it down.

After-call note and handoff template

A consistent record is what makes the handoff reviewable. Use it as the agreed call record or handoff for each call; enter it in your CRM only where the handler has authorized access (see how account work is set up). Keep the appointment date and window separate from the outcome, so a request is never read as a confirmed booking.

CALLER: [name] | CALLBACK: [number, read back] TYPE: new lead / existing customer PROPERTY: residential / commercial ADDRESS: [street, ZIP] AREA CHECK: in / out / borderline PROBLEM (caller’s words): [verbatim] DURATION / LOCATION: [how long, where on property] URGENCY: routine / priority / safety-routed ACCESS: [gate code, pets, parking] APPOINTMENT: [date + window, if any] OUTCOME: booked/rescheduled (confirmed) / appointment or change requested (pending) / cancellation completed (authorized) / callback / escalated / declined NEXT: [next owner, action, approved due time] PROMISED: [exact commitment made to the caller] ESCALATED TO: [name, method, time] HANDLER: [initials, time]

The line that matters most is PROMISED. Most morning friction comes from a commitment made on the phone that nobody else could see.

Quality-assurance checklist

Score a handful of calls a week against the same list, marking items that don’t apply to a call as not applicable. Never delay safety routing to finish intake. For a safety-routed, declined or disconnected call, record only the information available and the action taken. Fix whichever line fails most often before adding anything new to the script.

  • Answered in the company’s name, with a first name given.
  • Callback number captured early and read back.
  • Service area checked before any time was offered.
  • Problem recorded in the caller’s own words, with no pest identified by the handler.
  • New versus existing customer established; the record checked where authorized account access is available, otherwise the caller’s stated status noted.
  • Residential or commercial captured and routed correctly.
  • No price quoted outside the written boundary; no prohibited promise made.
  • Safety questions asked; any safety call routed to 911 or Poison Help and documented.
  • Any confirmed or requested day, window and address repeated back with its status stated; access notes captured.
  • Call record or handoff completed: truthful confirmed or pending status, the relevant details, the next owner and the exact promise made.

Five test calls to pressure-test the script

Illustrative examples

These scenarios are hypothetical and written for training — they are not real Callvix calls, clients or results. Run them as authorized internal tests to your own line, using fictional details, and follow any applicable call-recording and consent rules in your state.

Test 1 · Straightforward new lead

“I’ve got ants in the kitchen, can someone come out?”

In-area residential caller, standard service. Watch for: callback number read back, ZIP captured, area checked before a slot is offered, the problem written verbatim rather than labelled, and the outcome stated truthfully: confirmed only after an authorized reservation, otherwise a clearly pending request.

Test 2 · Priority, not an emergency

“There’s a wasp nest right over my front door.”

Time-sensitive but not a safety event. Watch for: the earliest authorized slot offered (or, if none can be reserved, a clearly pending request or callback) and a clear dispatch note — without inflating it into an emergency or waking a technician.

Test 3 · Outside the service area

“We’re just the other side of the county line.”

Nearby but excluded ZIP. Watch for: the area check happening before a time is offered, and the decline or referral policy followed accurately and politely.

Test 4 · Pricing pressure

“Just give me a number — how much for the whole house?”

Caller pushes for a firm quote. Watch for: the handler staying inside the written pricing boundary and escalating rather than inventing a figure.

Test 5 · Safety / off-script

“My little one got into the bait station under the sink.”

Suspected exposure. Watch for: booking stops immediately, the caller is routed to 911 or Poison Help 1-800-222-1222, no advice is given, and the call is documented and reported the same day.

Frequently asked questions

Should a call handler ever identify the pest for the caller?

No. Record the problem in the caller’s own words and leave identification to the company’s authorized pest professional. A phone identification can be wrong, can set the wrong expectation, and can lead to the wrong service being booked.

What should the script do if a caller describes a pesticide exposure or a medical emergency?

Stop the booking flow and direct the caller to the right authority: 911 for anything life-threatening, or the national Poison Help line at 1-800-222-1222 (poison.org) for a suspected pesticide exposure that is not immediately life-threatening. Do not give medical, chemical or treatment advice. Document the routing.

Should the script quote a price?

Only inside limits you set in writing. A workable limit is to let a handler give a published starting range for standard residential work and escalate anything outside it — square-footage outliers, commercial accounts, termite or wildlife work, and any warranty question.

How long should a pest control intake call take?

There is no universal target, and a good call is not the same as a fast call. Use the required-fields list as the standard instead: an ordinary eligible call is complete when the callback number, address and ZIP, service-area confirmation, problem description, property type, urgency and the promise made are captured where possible. Never delay safety routing to finish intake. For a safety-routed, declined or disconnected call, record only the information available and the action taken.

Can we use this script with an answering service or an AI receptionist?

Yes. The script is written to be executed by whoever answers — an in-house CSR, an answering service, or an automated system. The rules that matter are the same in all three cases: service-area check before booking, no diagnosis, defined pricing limits, and a named escalation path. Adapt it, then test it

A script is only as good as the four decisions behind it. Once yours are written down, run the five test calls above and score them against the checklist — that is usually where the gap between the script and what actually happens on the phone shows up.

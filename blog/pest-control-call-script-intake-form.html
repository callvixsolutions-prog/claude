Pest Control Call Script and Customer Intake Form: Free Template

Callvix Solutions14 min readPublished September 2026

By Callvix Solutions. Disclosure: Callvix runs a human-fronted call-handling and booking service for U.S. pest-control companies, so we have a commercial interest here. This template is free to copy and adapt whether or not you ever hire us.

What this is: a complete intake script for inbound pest control calls — greeting, qualification, service-area check, safety boundaries, booking limits, escalation and after-call notes — plus a printable one-page form your team can keep by the phone. Adapt every rule to your own service area, licensing and booking policy before you use it.

How to use this script

Treat it as a decision structure, not a word-for-word recital. The wording in the blue boxes is a starting point you should rewrite in your own voice; the parts that matter are the order of the questions and the boundaries around what a handler may promise.

Read it once end to end, then fill in the four blanks in the next section. A script without those four decisions filled in will produce confident-sounding answers that your field team has to undo the next morning.

Fill these in before the phone rings

Everything below depends on four decisions that only you can make. Write them down and give them to whoever answers.

  1. Service area. The ZIP codes, cities or radius you actually serve, and what to do with an address just outside them — decline politely, refer, or capture for review.
  2. Booking authority. Which calendar slots a handler may offer without asking you, and which requests must be escalated instead of booked.
  3. Pricing boundaries. What a handler may say about price — a published starting range, or nothing at all beyond “a technician will confirm pricing.”
  4. Escalation contact. One named person, one contact method, and what counts as urgent enough to use it after hours.

If you have not settled the coverage windows those rules apply to, our after-hours coverage and escalation plan walks through choosing them from your own call data.

The call script, step by step

1. Opening greeting — in your company’s name

Say“Thank you for calling [Company Name], this is [First Name]. How can I help you today?”

Answer in the company’s name, not a generic greeting. If an outside service answers for you, the greeting should be indistinguishable from your own office. Give a first name — callers with an urgent pest problem settle faster when they know who they are talking to.

2. New caller or existing customer?

Ask“Have we been out to your property before, or is this your first time calling us?”

This single question routes the whole call. An existing account may be covered by a re-service policy and should never be booked as a new one-time job by mistake. Search the record by phone number and service address before going further.

  • New lead
  • Existing customer
  • Account found in system

3. Caller name and a confirmed callback number

Ask“Can I start with your name and the best number to reach you on, in case we get cut off?”
Read back“Let me repeat that back — [number]. Is that right?”

Take this first, before the problem description. If the call drops mid-sentence you still have a lead. Always read the number back digit by digit; a transposed digit is the most common reason a morning callback never happens.

  • Caller name
  • Callback number (read back)
  • Email, if offered

4. Service address, ZIP code and service-area check

Ask“What’s the address where you’re seeing the problem, including the ZIP code?”

Check the address against your service area before offering any appointment time. Offering a slot and withdrawing it is worse than a polite decline, and a job booked well outside your route costs more than the call was worth. If the address is borderline, capture it and escalate rather than guessing.

  • Street address
  • ZIP code
  • In area / out of area / borderline

5. The problem, in the caller’s own words

Ask“Tell me what you’re seeing — where in the property, and roughly how long it’s been going on?”

Write down what the caller says, not what you think it means. “Small black ants along the kitchen counter for two weeks” is useful to a technician. “Ant problem” is not, and “odorous house ants” is a guess the handler is not qualified to make.

  • Pest/problem as described
  • Location on property
  • How long / how many seen
  • Previous treatment attempts

Hard rule: no diagnosis, no treatment advice

Whoever answers the phone must not identify the pest, recommend a pesticide or treatment, estimate how severe an infestation is, or give medical advice — even when the caller pushes for an answer. Identification belongs to a licensed technician who can see the property.

A safe deflection: “I don’t want to guess at that over the phone — the technician will identify it properly when they’re on site and explain the options then.”

6. Residential or commercial

Ask“And is this a home, or a business property?”

Commercial work usually carries different pricing, scheduling, documentation and sometimes licensing requirements. Many companies route every commercial enquiry to an owner or manager rather than booking it at intake. Decide which you do, and put it in the script.

  • Residential
  • Commercial
  • Multi-unit / HOA

7. Urgency and the safety boundary

Ask“Is anyone in the home having a reaction, or is anything making the property unsafe right now?”

This question separates ordinary urgency — which is a scheduling problem — from a safety situation, which is not a booking at all. Most urgent-sounding calls are simply priority bookings: a stinging-insect nest by a door, or rodents in a food business before an inspection.

  • Routine
  • Priority / time-sensitive
  • Safety — stop and route

Safety routing — stop the booking flow

If a caller describes a medical emergency, a severe allergic reaction to a sting, or a suspected pesticide poisoning, stop taking the booking and direct them to the right authority:

911 for anything life-threatening — collapse, seizure, trouble breathing, or someone who cannot be woken.

For a suspected pesticide exposure that is not immediately life-threatening, the national Poison Help line at 1-800-222-1222, or the online tool at poison.org.

Do not give medical, chemical or treatment advice. EPA advises reading and following the pesticide label’s instructions and safety warnings, so an intake handler should never improvise around a product label. Document who you routed the caller to, and tell your manager the same day.

8. Booking authority and pricing boundaries

Say (if you publish a range)“For a standard [service] on a home that size, our pricing usually starts around [range]. The technician confirms the exact price on site before any work starts.”
Say (if you do not)“I’m not able to quote that one myself — I’ll have [name] confirm the pricing with you. Can I book the visit provisionally in the meantime?”

Never invent a number. The common “never promise” list: a firm quote for non-standard work, a named technician, a guaranteed arrival time you cannot staff, a warranty decision, or a same-day visit outside your booking rules.

9. Booking the appointment

Offer“I have [day] between [window], or [day] between [window]. Which works better?”
Confirm“You’re booked for [day, date, window] at [address]. You’ll get a confirmation by [text/email]. Is there gate access, a dog, or anything the technician should know before they arrive?”

Offer two concrete windows rather than an open question — it books faster and protects route density. Always close by repeating the day, window and address, and capture access notes while the caller is still on the line.

  • Date + arrival window
  • Access notes / gate code
  • Pets on site
  • Confirmation method

10. Manager escalation

Say“That one needs [name] to confirm for you. I’m going to take the details now and have them call you back on [number] — is that the best number?”

Escalate rather than improvise whenever the call falls outside written rules: commercial accounts, termite or wildlife work, warranty and complaint calls, pricing outliers, and any address on the edge of the service area. Capture the full details first so the manager is not starting from zero, then log who was contacted and when.

11. Existing customers: reschedule and cancellation

Reschedule“I can move that for you. I have [option] or [option] — which suits?”
Cancellation“I’m sorry to hear that. Can I ask what’s prompting the change? I’ll pass this to [name] and they’ll follow up with you today.”

Rescheduling is usually safe to handle at intake. Cancellations generally are not: capture the reason in the caller’s words and route them to an owner or manager the same day rather than processing the cancellation on the call. A recorded reason is the most useful retention data most companies never collect.

After-call CRM note template

A consistent note format is what makes the morning review possible. Paste this into your CRM’s appointment or account note and fill it in on every call, whichever system you run — see how we work inside the system your office already uses.

CALLER: [name] | CALLBACK: [number, read back] TYPE: new lead / existing customer PROPERTY: residential / commercial ADDRESS: [street, ZIP] AREA CHECK: in / out / borderline PROBLEM (caller’s words): [verbatim] DURATION / LOCATION: [how long, where on property] URGENCY: routine / priority / safety-routed ACCESS: [gate code, pets, parking] OUTCOME: booked / callback / escalated / declined PROMISED: [exact commitment made to the caller] ESCALATED TO: [name, method, time] HANDLER: [initials, time]

The line that matters most is PROMISED. Most morning friction comes from a commitment made on the phone that nobody else could see.

Quality-assurance checklist

Score a handful of calls a week against the same list. Fix whichever line fails most often before adding anything new to the script.

  • Answered in the company’s name, with a first name given.
  • Callback number captured early and read back.
  • Service area checked before any time was offered.
  • Problem recorded in the caller’s own words, with no pest identified by the handler.
  • New versus existing customer established and the record checked.
  • Residential or commercial captured and routed correctly.
  • No price quoted outside the written boundary; no prohibited promise made.
  • Safety questions asked; any safety call routed to 911 or Poison Help and documented.
  • Appointment day, window and address repeated back; access notes captured.
  • CRM note completed, including the exact promise made.

Five test calls to pressure-test the script

Illustrative examples

These scenarios are hypothetical and written for training — they are not real Callvix calls, clients or results. Run them as authorized internal tests to your own line, using fictional details, and follow any applicable call-recording and consent rules in your state.

Test 1 · Straightforward new lead

“I’ve got ants in the kitchen, can someone come out?”

In-area residential caller, standard service. Watch for: callback number read back, ZIP captured, area checked before a slot is offered, and the problem written verbatim rather than labelled.

Test 2 · Priority, not an emergency

“There’s a wasp nest right over my front door.”

Time-sensitive but not a safety event. Watch for: earliest approved slot offered and a clear dispatch note — without inflating it into an emergency or waking a technician.

Test 3 · Outside the service area

“We’re just the other side of the county line.”

Nearby but excluded ZIP. Watch for: the area check happening before a time is offered, and the decline or referral policy followed accurately and politely.

Test 4 · Pricing pressure

“Just give me a number — how much for the whole house?”

Caller pushes for a firm quote. Watch for: the handler staying inside the written pricing boundary and escalating rather than inventing a figure.

Test 5 · Safety / off-script

“My little one got into the bait station under the sink.”

Suspected exposure. Watch for: booking stops immediately, the caller is routed to 911 or Poison Help 1-800-222-1222, no advice is given, and the call is documented and reported the same day.

Frequently asked questions

Should a call handler ever identify the pest for the caller?

No. Record the problem in the caller’s own words and let a licensed technician identify the pest on site. A phone identification can be wrong, can set the wrong expectation, and can lead to the wrong service being booked.

What should the script do if a caller describes a pesticide exposure or a medical emergency?

Stop the booking flow and direct the caller to the right authority: 911 for anything life-threatening, or the national Poison Help line at 1-800-222-1222 (poison.org) for a suspected pesticide exposure that is not immediately life-threatening. Do not give medical, chemical or treatment advice. Document the routing.

Should the script quote a price?

Only inside limits you set in writing. Most companies let a handler give a published starting range for standard residential work and escalate anything outside it — square-footage outliers, commercial accounts, termite or wildlife work, and any warranty question.

How long should a pest control intake call take?

There is no universal target, and a good call is not the same as a fast call. Use the required-fields list as the standard instead: a call is complete when the callback number, address and ZIP, service-area confirmation, problem description, property type, urgency and the promise made are all captured.

Can we use this script with an answering service or an AI receptionist?

Yes. The script is written to be executed by whoever answers — an in-house CSR, an answering service, or an automated system. The rules that matter are the same in all three cases: service-area check before booking, no diagnosis, defined pricing limits, and a named escalation path. Adapt it, then test it

A script is only as good as the four decisions behind it. Once yours are written down, run the five test calls above and score them against the checklist — that is usually where the gap between the script and what actually happens on the phone shows up.
